# Summary

Developer productivity has several dimensions: satisfaction and well-being, performance, activity, communication and collaboration, and efficiency and flow. Activity is the easiest to count and covers only one of them. A platform often improves flow by removing waiting, and that benefit does not appear in a count of commits or requests.

Quantitative evidence is counted or timed. It scales to all users and shows size and trend, but it does not explain a cause. A raw count also depends on usage, so a rate with a denominator, such as tickets per 100 sessions, is comparable between months. Qualitative evidence comes from interviews, observed use and coded survey comments. It explains why users struggle, and it covers only the users who answered. A report names who is missing from its data.

Numbers must be read together. A fall in support tickets can mean fewer problems, and it can mean that users give up and stop asking. A completion rate that falls at the same time points to the second reading. A survey that shows unclear error messages and users who bypass the platform supports it.

A measure that becomes a target is gamed. Ranking people by an activity count rewards extra activity and can discourage work that helps others. A balanced set of measures, a clear purpose for each one and the use of measures to find system problems reduce the harm.

A before-and-after comparison shows that numbers differ. Another change in the same period, called a confounder, can produce the same effect, and the comparison cannot separate them without finer data or a comparison group. A report states the evidence, the limits and the changes it could not separate.

A survey asks one concrete question at a time, keeps the same scale between rounds and reports the distribution, the number of answers and the response rate. The answers describe the people who answered, so the report names who is missing, and results for small groups are not shown.

Qualitative evidence explains the numbers. Comments are grouped into themes, and a theme count shows how often a topic came up in the answers, not how many users have the problem. Interviews add depth. Triangulation checks a conclusion against several kinds of evidence, which makes a story stronger but does not prove a cause.

Key facts:

- A ticket count falls when users give up, so read it with the completion rate.
- A rate needs a denominator.
- A confounder is another change in the same period that could explain the result.
- A metric used as a target stops describing what it was meant to describe.
- A survey report states the distribution, the answers and the response rate.
- A theme count is a share of the answers, not a share of the users.
